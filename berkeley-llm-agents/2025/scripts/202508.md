Now let me go into the methods. Before we get there, I want to remind you that, when we're talking about the methods, there are two important aspects we needed to think about. One is where do the problems come from and how are we going to have an agent that learns to solve those problems?

We trained several models. One model is a formalized model. It takes as input a problem described in natural language and outputs a Lean formalization. This is based on the idea that we are going to lean onto human-defined problems instead of inventing our own problems, and we are going to try to translate them in Lean, because they all exist in natural language, but they don't exist in it. Trying to do that with humans doesn't scale at all, takes a long time. Instead, we want to try to train a model that can do this automatically. That's the formalized model.

We also have a prover model. The architecture of a prover model is that it takes this—if you remember in my demonstration before, this yellow box, the Tactic state, the Lean state that tells you what do you know now, what do you need to prove. This is the thing we're going to send as input to the prover model. Then the prover model is going to sample n continuations for the proof. This n-thing you might consider potentially part of the proof—alternatives. Then you take this prover model and you put it in the AlphaZero search. So you're going to search over actions which are basically one line in the proof. Each time you add one line in the proof, you send that to Lean. Lean tells you, "Oh, what do I know now? What do I need to prove now?" That's basically how the search works. If you know a little bit about AlphaZero and MCTS-like search, they try to exploit what the networks tell you—the neural networks tell you. They exploit the high-prior and high-value paths. But it has a mechanism also to explore low-visited paths. For instance, that's how Move 37 in AlphaGo is discovered: it might have a low prior, it might not be considered the right move by the network, but it was still going to try to visit it in case it might be an interesting move.

Now let me put this thing together in the pipeline. Step 1, we do auto formalization. We train a formalization model and auto formalize human creative problems. This is really the answer to the question, where do the problems come from around human mathematics. Here I want to highlight a few advantages of using formalization of the problem. First is that when you send a problem to Lean and Lean says this is a valid syntax, it means that your question makes sense. It's not something that doesn't make sense. So it's either true or false. You're generating something that is either true or false. The problems might not be interesting. They might be trivially true or trivially false. But they are either true or false. One way to see this is you have the human problem in the middle and you try to generate Lean variants of it. If your model is really good, then you will do the perfect translation. But maybe also it will generate problems that look like that problem. When we have this formalization model, the way we use it is that we were able to gather about a million informal problems. There is a lot of problems to train for the IMO, about a million of them. Now, we need to train the prover to be good at proving. That's why you don't really want to be completely in the AlphaZero philosophy, where you really start from scratch. Here, you want to be pragmatic and you want to start with something that already can do a few things. So you take a pretrained model. In our case, it was pretrained on a lot of code. The next step is you're going to train the prover model supervised on Mathlib. Mathlib has about 100,000 definitions, 200,000 theorems, 300,000 lines of proof. Basically, you are really going to do supervised learning. You are going to place yourself inside of a human proof. You're going to look at what is the Lean state there. Then you're going to look at what the human said was the next step in the proof, and you're going to train the model towards it. In some sense, very similar to regular pretraining but you get much more information from it. When you've done that, your prover model has learned a really good prior over the actions to take.

Unfortunately, Mathlib is about, I don't know, but especially if you want to do the IMO, Mathlib doesn't contain much IMO problems. It's all about research math. That's why you start to have to discover new knowledge about how to solve those IMO problems. That's where you enter the next stage of the AlphaZero reinforcement learning stage. You are going to train the prover model by RL. The recipe is very simple. It's the RL recipe. For each formal problem, you are going to generate experience of either proving or disproving that problem by searching over steps. Once you've come up with what you think is a proof, you're going to use Lean to verify the proofs. Lean is going to say, "This is not a proof. This is a proof." For the things that are proofs, you are going to reinforce your prover network on those proofs. What happens is that, at the very beginning, the prover model is able to prove the very easiest problems, really, really easy problems. It's going to learn from that experience and climb the next rung on the ladder that leads all the way to IMO strength. It's going to be able to then solve a few more problems a bit harder. All these proving strategies for the IMO, this is completely discovered by the model by itself.
