Finally, let's talk about retrieval-augmented generation. We probably have a lot of knowledge, much bigger than what we can store in the parameters of a large language model. Retrieval-augmented generation is a very popular technique. We don't need the LLM to remember all the knowledge in its parameters. Instead, we can have a database to store all that information. For example, these five important papers authored by Geoffrey Hinton—we don't need to remember everything here in the weights of a large language model, but we put it somewhere else in the database. When we have a new prompt, we retrieve only the useful, related information from the database and then concatenate them together and feed to the language model, and then finally get the results.

For example, "What protects the digestive system against infections?" Here, it has retrieved the piece of text from the database: "In the stomach, gastric acid and proteases serve as chemical defenses against infectious pathogens," and the model will say, through the large language model, "gastric acid and proteases." This is another way to add more knowledge, because the LLM's knowledge is easily outdated. You train a model, that's the cutoff time, and all new information after that particular date will be outdated. For example, asking who the CEO of Twitter is—as the knowledge cutoff is September 2021, it will say Jack Dorsey, which is no longer the truth. With retrieval-augmented generation, you just need to update the database; you don't need to update the large language model.

How does that work? It consists of several components. It has an embedding model. You have the embedding model—basically, you want to turn those documents into vectors, vectorize everything so that we can compare. People use this MTEB benchmark to evaluate the embedding models. If you want to build a project for your final project, feel free to check out some of the good embedding models from MTEB benchmark.

You also have a retriever to fetch the most relevant document to match your query. This is your document, this is your query—you want to make sure they can match. Then there is a reranker to determine how relevant the retrieved document for the question is on hand, providing a relevance score for each one. Finally, you can combine your question with the data from the database, concatenate them together, and feed to the large language model to get the best results. That way, you can always have up-to-date information related to your task.
